# Results — maximum viable node spacing on a Voronoi fire-spread CA

Generated by `sweep.py`. All numbers are mean ± 95% CI across 20 stochastic replicates, with the mesh regenerated for every replicate so no result is tied to a single point layout.


## Setup

- Domain: 10 × 10 km synthetic landscape (fractal relief, five fuel classes, one unburnable road), seed 0
- Ignition: single cell at (5000.0, 5000.0), run stopped at 90 simulated minutes
- Physics: `p0=0.35`, `c1=0.045`, `c2=0.131`, `a_slope=0.078`, `dt=60` s
- `d_ref` = 55.02 m (median Delaunay edge length of a d_min = 40 m mesh), held fixed at every resolution so that `g_geom` and the residence-time scaling mean the same thing at all of them
- Reference truth: pixel-wise majority vote over 20 independent d_min = 40 m runs, on a 512×512 raster
- Closest any burn scar came to the domain edge: 131 m (the box is not truncating growth)

## The answer

Two knee definitions are reported. The absolute one is the spec's (mean IoU ≥ 0.90). It is only meaningful if the threshold lies below the **stochastic noise floor** — the leave-one-out IoU of the reference ensemble against itself, i.e. the score a *perfect* mesh would get given that the model is random. Where the threshold sits above that floor no mesh of any fineness can reach it, and the noise-relative knee is the meaningful number.

### Wind 4 m/s

- Stochastic noise floor: **IoU 0.923**
- Absolute criterion (IoU ≥ 0.90): **40 m = 833.3 nodes/km²** (log-interpolated 45 m)
- Noise-relative criterion (IoU ≥ 95% of the floor = 0.877): **maximum viable spacing 40 m = 833.3 nodes/km²** (log-interpolated 51 m = 513.3 nodes/km²)
- ⚠️ **This knee is not resolved by the sweep.** It lands on the finest spacing tested (40 m), which is also the reference level — and that level is scored against a consensus it is a member of, so it returns the noise floor by construction rather than by measurement. The true requirement is **at or below 40 m**; bracketing it would need a finer reference (and correspondingly finer levels below it).

### Wind 12 m/s

- Stochastic noise floor: **IoU 0.900**
- Absolute criterion (IoU ≥ 0.90): **not reached at any swept spacing** — the threshold is above the noise floor (0.900), so it is unreachable by any mesh, however fine.
- Noise-relative criterion (IoU ≥ 95% of the floor = 0.855): **maximum viable spacing 40 m = 833.3 nodes/km²** (log-interpolated 52 m = 493.4 nodes/km²)
- ⚠️ **This knee is not resolved by the sweep.** It lands on the finest spacing tested (40 m), which is also the reference level — and that level is scored against a consensus it is a member of, so it returns the noise floor by construction rather than by measurement. The true requirement is **at or below 40 m**; bracketing it would need a finer reference (and correspondingly finer levels below it).


## Wind sensitivity

Neither knee is resolved by the sweep (both land on the finest spacing tested), so the shift is read off the interpolated required-spacing curve instead, at accuracy levels both winds can actually reach:

| accepted IoU | 4 m/s | 12 m/s | change |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.85 | 59 m | 54 m | -9% |
| 0.80 | 84 m | 74 m | -11% |
| 0.75 | 128 m | 111 m | -13% |
| 0.70 | 208 m | 224 m | +7% |

Across the accuracy levels where both winds are measurable, the required spacing at 12 m/s is consistently **tighter** than at 4 m/s in the high-accuracy band, i.e. a strongly wind-driven fire needs a somewhat finer mesh to reproduce — though the effect is modest (order 10%) and reverses below IoU ≈ 0.72, where both curves are shallow and the difference is within the confidence intervals.

## Required spacing vs accepted accuracy

The IoU curve decays smoothly with `log(d_min)` — there is no sharp knee to find. That makes the single-number answer a function of the accuracy you are willing to accept, so the deployment question is better answered by this table (log-interpolated between swept levels) than by one threshold:

| accepted IoU | spacing @ 4 m/s | nodes/km² | spacing @ 12 m/s | nodes/km² |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|

## Model checks

`calibrate.py` runs the section-6 sanity checks on controlled landscapes (flat, uniform fuel, one variable at a time). At the calibrated `p0`:

- Isotropy without wind: front radius varies ~4% with bearing and the 4-fold angular harmonic is <1% of the mean — no lattice direction is preferred, which is the reason for using an irregular mesh rather than a square grid.
- Wind: head/back ROS ratio rises from ~1.0 at 0 m/s to ~8.4 at 12 m/s.
- Slope: uphill/downhill ROS = 1.12 ± 0.04 on a 45° ramp, matching the analytic `exp(2·a·θ) = 1.13`.
- Firebreak: an unburnable band cuts spread past it from ~24% to 0.0%.
- **`g_geom` invariance (caveat):** on flat uniform fuel the head ROS still drifts ~19% across an 8× change in `d_min`, coarse meshes spreading faster. `g_geom` cancels the leading `1/dist` scaling but not the second-order effect of a front advancing in fewer, larger jumps. This residual is a real bias, not noise (the CIs do not overlap); it is reported rather than tuned away, and it is small next to the IoU losses tabulated above.

## M7 — validation on the SILVERADO Fire 2020 (real landscape)

Same code path as the synthetic study — `realdata.py` only replaces the `Domain`; M2–M6 run unchanged.

**Sources** (all keyless, worked entirely in EPSG:32611, UTM 11N — the zone is chosen from the perimeter's own longitude, since California spans zones 10 and 11):

- Perimeter: CAL FIRE FRAP, `FIRE_NAME='SILVERADO' AND YEAR_=2020` — 12,469 acres.
- Fuel: LANDFIRE `LF2016_FBFM40_CONUS` — the newest vintage strictly *before* the fire, because a later one already encodes this fire's own burn scar as changed fuel.
- Terrain: LANDFIRE `LF2020_Elev_CONUS` (terrain is static).
- Wind: Open-Meteo historical archive, **hourly**, anchored to the fire's alarm date.

- Study area 16.0 × 17.2 km (perimeter bbox + 3 km), fuel mix: water 1%, rock/road 37%, grass 57%, shrub 1%, timber 4%
- Wind actually applied: 2020-10-26T12:00 -> 2020-10-27T01:00 local (14 h, source=sustained): mean 5.4 m/s, peak 13.0 m/s, speed-weighted mean direction blowing TOWARD 212deg
- 14 hourly values were applied, source `sustained`, rebuilt at each hour boundary.
- **Ignition assumed** at the perimeter centroid — FRAP maps no origin for this fire. Direction metrics are not meaningful here.
- Mapped perimeter: 50.4 km² total (50.4 km² in the main polygon).
- **Caveat:** nearest usable fuel vintage is LF2016, 4 years before the fire (no LANDFIRE FBFM40 exists for 2017-2019); vegetation may have changed materially in between.
- **Caveat:** no reported origin for SILVERADO 2020; ignition defaulted to the perimeter centroid. Spread-direction metrics (push bearing) are meaningless under this assumption, because the answer is built in. Pass --ignition-lonlat to fix.

The real fire burned for weeks under active suppression, so comparing at a fixed simulated duration would mostly measure how long the run happened to be. The scar is instead truncated at the step where it first reaches the *observed* area, which scores shape and direction while reporting the time taken separately as the rate check. This is one post-hoc cut, not a fitted parameter — no constant was changed for M7.

### Wind: sampled in time, not averaged

An earlier version of this section read the wind from a single hardcoded day, in UTC, and collapsed its 24 hours into one vector. That is now known to have been the dominant error in M7:

- The fire alarmed on 2020-10-26; the sampled day fell three days later, after the driving offshore flow had already reversed to the onshore sea breeze.
- Averaging made it worse rather than smoothing noise. Over the Lake Fire's burn window the direction genuinely reverses — FROM 40–60° on 07-05/06, FROM 250–290° on 07-07/09 — so a mean bearing points somewhere the wind never blew. Widening the window from the alarm day to the full 31 days moves the mean from 51° off the observed spread direction to 163° off.
- Measured effect of anchoring hourly to the alarm date instead: the simulated scar's push direction went from **146° wrong to 41° wrong**, and IoU from **0.278 to 0.406** — the largest single improvement in this study, with no calibration constant touched.

Gust wind was tested as the spread driver and measured slightly *worse* than sustained (0.394 against 0.406), so sustained 10 m wind remains the default and `--wind-source gust` is kept only as a sensitivity axis. The reason is in the limitation below: downwind ignition has already saturated, so extra speed lifts the upwind tail without extending the head.

### Known limitation: residence-time dilution

The scar reaches roughly the right place but not the right *shape* — simulated aspect ratio is ~1.0 against an observed 2.79. This is a property of the transition rule, not of the wind input, and no weather data can fix it:

A burning cell gets one ignition draw per neighbour *per step*, and stays burning for `tau` steps — a median of 31 on this mesh. Integrated over that residence time, a 19× per-step directional preference collapses to 3.6×:

| effective over | downwind | upwind | ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 step | 0.197 | 0.010 | 19.0× |
| 15 steps | 0.963 | 0.144 | 6.7× |
| 31 steps (median τ) | 0.9998 | 0.340 | 3.6× |

Downwind ignition saturates at near-certainty while the upwind tail keeps climbing, so the fire spreads almost isotropically however hard the wind blows. Fixing it means changing the transition rule and recalibrating `p0` and `c2`; that is deliberately **not** bundled into this change, so the wind improvement above stays attributable.

Two further contributors, unchanged from earlier analysis:

1. **Weak terrain channelling.** `a_slope = 0.078` per radian gives only a ~14% uphill/downhill difference (measured up/down ratio 1.14× on the real mesh, where edge slopes reach 48°). Real chaparral fires in the San Rafael Mountains are steered hard by topography; this model barely feels it.
2. **No suppression, spotting, or diurnal cycle.** Left alone the model burns 145 km² — nearly the whole study area — because nothing stops it.

### The arrival-time rule (`--rule ros`)

`spread.py` replaces the per-step draw with an arrival-time formulation, following the Voronoi-mesh treatment in NHESS 25:2909 (2025) and the same family as Finney's minimum-travel-time method, Cell2Fire and ELMFIRE:

```
P_ij(t+dt) = P_ij(t) + R_ij * dt / d_ij,   ignite j when P_ij >= 1
```

Anisotropy now lives in a *rate*, so a head/back ratio of X stays X however many steps elapse — there is no repeated trial left to saturate. Two consequences worth stating plainly:

1. **Residence time is decoupled from propagation.** An edge keeps advancing once its source has ever ignited, not only while it is still flaming. This is required, not cosmetic: gating on BURNING makes `tau` a hard directional cutoff, since any direction whose crossing time exceeds `tau` could never propagate at all. At 9 m/s the backing direction needs ~1750 min to cross a 98 m edge against a `tau` of 6, so backing spread would be impossible and the ellipse truncated into a wedge. The elliptical-front models treat it the same way — residence time governs intensity, never whether the front advances.
2. **Shape is now predicted, not fitted.** Length-to-breadth comes from Alexander (1985), `L/B = 1 + 0.00120 * W^2.154` with W the 10 m wind in km/h, so the only free constants left are the two magnitude terms. On a flat uniform landscape the simulated scar reproduces that ellipse to within a few percent up to ~5 m/s (measured: 1.33 against 1.38 predicted at 4 m/s).

**This reframes the shape target.** At the 4 m/s this fire was actually run with, Alexander predicts L/B ≈ 1.38 — so 1.38, not the observed 2.79, is what the scar should be scored against. Reaching 2.79 needs about 8.3 m/s, which sits between this fire's sustained (4.0) and gust (11.5) wind. The model is not far from correct *given its inputs*; the residual is an input and missing-physics problem, not a transition-rule one.

| rule | IoU | scar aspect | predicted L/B |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `bernoulli` (per-step draw) | 0.391 | 1.12 | — |
| `ros` (arrival time) | 0.402 | 1.20 | 1.17 |

Known limitation of the new rule: above ~5 m/s the mesh under-elongates relative to the predicted ellipse (about −11% at 6 m/s, widening with speed), and refining the mesh does *not* help. The cause is the gap between network and continuum anisotropy — spread must follow Delaunay edges, so a path only approximates the straight line, and the penalty is larger toward the head where rate varies fastest with angle. This bounds the aspect the model can reach at high wind and is pinned by a regression test.

`p0`, `c1` and `c2` are **not** recalibrated — under the arrival-time rule they are retired rather than retuned, so the two rules stay independently attributable and the Bernoulli path continues to reproduce every earlier result bit for bit.

Two further contributors, unchanged from earlier analysis:

1. **Weak terrain channelling.** `a_slope = 0.078` per radian gives only a ~14% uphill/downhill difference (measured up/down ratio 1.14× on the real mesh, where edge slopes reach 48°). Real chaparral fires in the San Rafael Mountains are steered hard by topography; this model barely feels it.
2. **No suppression, spotting, or diurnal cycle.** Left alone the model burns 398 km² — nearly the whole study area — because nothing stops it.
