## 1. Lightning-caused wildfire: the magnitude

**Canada, general.** Environment and Climate Change Canada, citing Natural
Resources Canada data: lightning-started fires are **45% of all Canadian
forest fires** but **81% of total area burned**, against an average of ~8,000
fires and ~2.3 million ha burned per year.
[canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/lightning/forest-fires.html](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/lightning/forest-fires.html)

That 81% is a long-run average, not a fixed constant — CBC reporting (citing
NRCan fire scientist Chelene Hanes, May 2024) puts lightning's share of a
given season's area burned as high as ~90%.
[cbc.ca/news/climate/canada-wildfire-starts-1.7219681](https://www.cbc.ca/news/climate/canada-wildfire-starts-1.7219681)
**Report the range (81–93%, see 2023 below) rather than one number** — it
moves with the year, not with a measurement error.

**The asymmetry is the important fact, not either number alone.** Lightning
fires are a minority of ignitions and a supermajority of the area burned,
because they start in remote, unsuppressed terrain and burn until weather
stops them, not until a crew arrives.

**2023 Canadian season — verified from the peer-reviewed retrospective.**
Jain et al., *"Drivers and Impacts of the Record-Breaking 2023 Wildfire
Season in Canada,"* Nature Communications (2024):
[nature.com/articles/s41467-024-51154-7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-51154-7)

- ~6,700 reported ignitions. **Lightning caused 59% of fires and 93% of area
  burned** (against a 1959–2015 average of 91% — so 2023 was an intense year
  even by the lightning-fire baseline, not an outlier mechanism).
- Fires that started on **four single days** — May 13, May 27, June 1, and
  July 5 — account for **30% of the entire year's area burned**. That is the
  lightning-outbreak dynamic in one statistic: a handful of dry-lightning
  events did most of the damage.
- **2023 had the third-lowest total lightning detection count since 1998.**
  The record season was driven by fuel dryness, not unusual lightning
  activity — a fact worth stating plainly because it undercuts any framing
  that implies "more lightning" caused 2023.
- **Area burned has two commonly cited figures, and the paper reconciles
  them.** 18.5 Mha is CIFFC's preliminary operational estimate, built from
  active-fire perimeters reported by agencies during the season. The paper's
  own satellite-derived National Burned Area Composite gives **~15 Mha** —
  about 4% of Canada's forest area, more than double the prior record (6.7 Mha,
  1989). **Use ~15 Mha as the rigorous number; cite 18.5 Mha only as the
  widely-quoted preliminary figure, with this caveat attached.**
- 79% of detected fires got a full-suppression response; the fires that were
  merely "modified and monitored" — i.e. left to burn, typically because they
  are remote — contributed **61% of the total area burned**. This is the
  mechanism behind the lightning/area-burned asymmetry made explicit for a
  single season.

**Quebec 2023 — verified directly from the operating agency.** SOPFEU
(Société de protection des forêts contre le feu), Quebec's fire agency:
[sopfeu.qc.ca/bilan](https://www.sopfeu.qc.ca/bilan/)

> "566 feux ont brûlé près de 1,1 million d'hectares en zone de protection
> intensive, et 147 feux ont rasé plus de 3,2 millions d'hectares en zone
> nordique, pour un total de 4,3 millions d'hectares, un record... la
> superficie brûlée cette année, causée à 99,9 % par la foudre, est plus
> élevée que la somme des superficies brûlées des 20 dernières années, toutes
> causes confondues."

**4.3 million hectares burned in Quebec in 2023 — a century record — 99.9% of
it attributable to lightning, exceeding the combined area burned of the
previous twenty years combined.** This is the single strongest sentence in
this document for a judge: it is regional, it is the demo box's own province,
and it is from the fire agency itself, not a secondary summary.

**The New York smoke event — verified.** Quebec smoke reached New York City
June 6–8, 2023, pushed south by an atypical coastal low near Prince Edward
Island (normally Quebec smoke tracks east, out to sea). NYC's Air Quality
Index peaked at **≥465** during the event.
Rault et al., *"Origin of Smoke in the Record-Breaking Air-Pollution Event in
New York, June 2023,"* Atmospheric Science Letters,
[doi.org/10.1002/asl.1306](https://doi.org/10.1002/asl.1306) (full text
paywalled; title, authorship, journal and headline findings confirmed via
independent search corroboration, not a direct read of the PDF). Corroborated
by [Wikipedia's 2023 Canadian wildfires article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Canadian_wildfires),
which independently states the same June 5–6 timing and the same
air-quality-alert outcome for New York, Connecticut and neighbouring states.

**Global.** No single agency source states a clean global lightning-vs-human
fraction the way NRCan does for Canada. A 2023 *Nature Geoscience* paper is
widely summarized as finding **~77% of burned area in extratropical intact
forests (2001–2020) is attributable to lightning** —
[nature.com/articles/s41561-023-01322-z](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-023-01322-z) —
but this document's author could not fetch the full text, only a
search-engine synthesis of it. **Treat 77% as unverified until someone reads
the paper directly**; do not put it on the site as a cited figure without
that read.

For the US: a Congressional Research Service brief states human-caused fires
are ~89% of fire count nationally but **lightning-caused fires are ~53% of
acreage burned** (2018–2022 average) —
[congress.gov/crs-product/IF10244](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF10244).
A commonly repeated web figure ("16% of US ignitions, 56% of acres") could
**not** be traced to a specific NIFC or USFS table and should be treated as
unverified folklore, not cited.

## 2. Ignition probability per strike

**The foundational model.** Wotton, B.M. and Martell, D.L. (2005), *"A
lightning fire occurrence model for Ontario,"* Canadian Journal of Forest
Research, 35(6), 1389–1401,
[doi.org/10.1139/x05-071](https://doi.org/10.1139/x05-071) (abstract open,
full text paywalled). Verified from the abstract directly: an index tracking
**duff moisture in sheltered areas near tree boles was the most significant
ignition predictor** in every region modelled, and positive-polarity
cloud-to-ground strikes had a positive influence on ignition probability in
most of the province (exception: the far northwest).

**Ecozone variation — verified by direct computation from primary data.**
Coogan, S.C.P., Cannon, A.J., and Flannigan, M.D. (2025), *"Lightning
ignition efficiency in Canadian forests,"* Fire Ecology, 21, article 34,
[doi.org/10.1186/s42408-025-00376-1](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42408-025-00376-1)
(open access). Using Canadian Lightning Detection Network flash counts and
National Fire Database ignitions, June–August 2001–2020:

| Ecozone | CG flashes | Lightning fires | Flashes per fire |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Montane Cordillera | 1,640,577 | 15,659 | **104.8 (~1 in 105)** |
| Boreal Plains | 8,272,056 | 9,901 | **835.5 (~1 in 836)** |

These ratios are computed directly from the paper's reported totals, not
read off a pre-stated ratio in a table — the paper's own tables/figures were
not fully retrieved, so treat the ~105/~836 figures as arithmetically
verified from stated raw counts rather than a quoted sentence. The order-of-
magnitude regional spread is the real finding either way: **ignition
efficiency per flash varies roughly 8-fold between these two ecozones**, and
the paper's Random Forest models rank **day-of-year, Drought Code, and Duff
Moisture Code** as the top three predictors of lightning efficiency in almost
every ecozone (exceptions: two BC coastal ecoprovinces where fine-fuel
moisture/precipitation dominate instead).

**No confirmed sharp threshold.** A 2025 *International Journal of Wildland
Fire* paper examined the widely-used "DMC > 20" ignition rule of thumb and
found **no consistent evidence of a threshold at DMC = 20** in either
lightning-fire ignition data or forest-floor consumption data — ignition
probability rises continuously with duff dryness rather than switching on at
a fixed value.
[frames.gov/catalog/70235](https://www.frames.gov/catalog/70235) (found via
secondary summary; recommend direct verification of exact wording before
quoting this one on a public page).

**Dry lightning.** The correctly-cited paper is Peterson, D., Wang, J.,
Ichoku, C., and Remer, L.A. (2010), *"Effects of lightning and other
meteorological factors on fire activity in the North American boreal
forest,"* Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics, 10, 6873–6888,
[acp.copernicus.org/articles/10/6873/2010/](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/10/6873/2010/)
(open access, abstract fetched directly). The verified sentence: **"dry
(in the absence of precipitation) lightning strikes account for only 20% of
the total lightning strikes, but are associated with (and likely cause) 40%
of the MODIS observed fire counts."** That is a 2x overrepresentation of dry
lightning among fire-causing strikes.

**This is not the same statistic as "dry lightning ignites 30–50% more
often."** That specific percentage — which appears in the design spec — could
not be located inside this paper's abstract, and no other primary source
supplied it. **Use the verified 20%-of-strikes / 40%-of-fires framing on the
site; do not state a "30–50% more likely" figure until someone reads the
paper's full body text and finds it.**

**Seasonality.** Coogan et al. (2025, above): lightning flash counts and fire
counts both rise through June and July, peaking around late July to early
August, then declining — consistent with peak fuel curing and peak dry-
lightning frequency coinciding.

## 3. Continuing current — the real discriminator

**Why peak current doesn't ignite fuel.** A return stroke is an impulsive
discharge lasting under 10 milliseconds — too brief to transfer enough
thermal energy to sustain combustion in duff or fine fuel, whatever its peak
amperage. Some cloud-to-ground flashes, though, have a **continuing-current
phase** lasting tens to hundreds of milliseconds after the return stroke,
carrying real charge across real time — long enough to heat fuel to
ignition. Verified directly from: Pérez-Invernón, F.J. et al. (2021),
*"Lightning-ignited wildfires and long continuing current lightning in the
Mediterranean Basin,"* Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics, 21, 17529–17557,
[acp.copernicus.org/articles/21/17529/2021/](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/21/17529/2021/)
(open access).

**The origin of the 40 ms threshold.** Fuquay, D.M. (1972), *"Lightning
discharges that caused forest fires,"* Journal of Geophysical Research,
77(12), 2156–2158,
[doi.org/10.1029/JC077i012p02156](https://doi.org/10.1029/JC077i012p02156).
This is the foundational, decades-old paper: of 16 documented discharges in
western Montana, 11 caused forest fires, and **all 11 fire-causing discharges
had a long-continuing-current phase of at least 40 ms**, versus only 2 of the
5 non-fire discharges. This document's author could not fetch the original
1972 text (pre-digital, likely archive-only) — the 40 ms figure is reported
here as it is consistently cited by later, fetchable papers (including the
Pérez-Invernón papers above), which raises confidence even without a direct
read.

**The 100–200 A magnitude figure is unverified.** No primary source was
found stating this specific amperage range as an ignition-capability
threshold. Secondary paraphrases disagree with each other — one gives
"200–800 A," another gives only "tens to hundreds of amperes" — which is not
a range, it's a sign nobody has traced this back to one number. **Do not
state "100–200 A" as a fact.** What would verify it: Fuquay (1972) directly,
or Rakov, V.A. and Uman, M.A., *Lightning: Physics and Effects* (Cambridge
University Press, 2003), chapter 4, the standard engineering reference for
continuing-current magnitude/duration statistics.

**The polarity correlation is real but weaker and messier than "80% vs 10%."**
This is the most important correction in this document. The specific figures
in the design spec — ~80% of positive strokes carrying long continuing
current against ~10% of negative — could not be traced to any primary source.
What the literature actually supports:

- Schultz, C.J. et al. (2019), *"Spatial, Temporal and Electrical
  Characteristics of Lightning in Reported Lightning-Initiated Wildfire
  Events,"* Fire, 2(2), 18,
  [doi.org/10.3390/fire2020018](https://doi.org/10.3390/fire2020018) (open
  access, fetched directly): earlier estimates put continuing current in
  **~20% of negative CG flashes**, with "more recent studies" raising that to
  **as high as 50%**. Positive flashes are historically assumed to carry
  continuing current more often, but this paper does not give a matching
  precise percentage for positive flashes.
- The same paper's own dataset is the important counterpoint: of 681 CG
  flashes matched to actual US wildfire ignition points, **90% were negative
  polarity** — because negative flashes vastly outnumber positive ones
  overall (positive flashes are typically only 4–20% of all CG activity by
  NLDN climatology). **In absolute terms, most fire-igniting flashes in this
  dataset were negative**, even though positive flashes may carry a higher
  per-flash probability of continuing current.
- Pérez-Invernón et al. (2021, above) report only **"a slightly higher
  probability of ignition for positive CG lightning flashes than for
  negative,"** citing Latham, D.J. and Williams, E.R. (2001), *"Lightning and
  forest fires,"* ch. 11 in *Forest Fires: Behavior and Ecological Effects*
  (Academic Press) — a chapter this document's author could not access
  directly (paywalled book), so "slightly higher" is as precise a figure as
  could be verified.

**What this means for copy:** positive polarity is a real, published,
directionally-correct proxy for ignition potential — but "positive strokes
are ~8x more likely to carry long continuing current" is not a number anyone
should say on stage. Say instead: *positive-polarity strokes are
statistically more likely to carry the long continuing current that actually
ignites fuel, but the effect size is not precisely quantified in the
literature, and most fire-igniting strokes in the largest matched dataset
were still negative, simply because negative strokes are so much more
common.* That sentence survives a subject-matter judge; "80% vs 10%" does
not, unless a primary citation for it is found.

## 4. Holdover

**The interval from strike to detectable fire is real, wide, and skewed —
and the 152,375 figure is real, but it is global, not Canada-specific.**
Moris, J.V. et al. (2023), *"A global database on holdover time of
lightning-ignited wildfires,"* Earth System Science Data, 15, 1151–1163,
[doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-1151-2023](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-1151-2023)
(open access, fetched and quoted directly). B.M. Wotton — the same Wotton as
the 2005 Ontario model — is a co-author, so this is a direct continuation of
the Canadian ignition-science lineage, not an unrelated dataset.

> "The first version of the database contains 42 frequency distributions
> built with data on more than 152,375 LIWs from 13 countries in five
> continents covering a time span from 1921 to 2020."

The 13 countries: Canada, the United States, Brazil, Spain, Italy,
Switzerland, Austria, France, Portugal, Greece, Finland, Russia, and
Australia — drawn from 29 published studies. **Say "global," not "Canadian,"
when citing this number.** Raw censored holdover intervals across the
underlying studies range from 1 minute to 87 days. The paper's own
qualitative summary: holdover "may range from a few minutes... to several
days and occasionally some weeks and even months."

**Gamma distribution — verified by citation, content by secondary summary.**
Moris, J.V., Ascoli, D., and Hunt, H. (2024), *"Survival functions of
holdover time of lightning-ignited wildfires,"* Electric Power Systems
Research, 231, article 110296,
[doi.org/10.1016/j.epsr.2024.110296](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.epsr.2024.110296).
The citation is confirmed via the VU Amsterdam institutional repository
record (journal, volume, article number, publisher, ISSN all match); the
full text sits behind a ScienceDirect wall this document's author could not
get past. Reported content (secondary summary, not independently read):
nine candidate distributions were fit to the same 152,375-record database
(exponential, chi-squared, log-normal, log-logistic, F, gamma, Weibull,
Pareto, Gompertz), and **gamma distributions gave the best fit**, with
separate survival functions derived for global, boreal, Mediterranean, and
temperate-coniferous biomes.

**No verified median or percentile.** Neither paper yielded a specific
median holdover time or a 90th-percentile figure this document's author
could confirm. **Report the shape of the distribution (wide, right-skewed,
gamma-family, 1 minute to 87 days observed) rather than inventing a median or
percentile.** What would verify a specific percentile: the full EPSR paper,
or the underlying Zenodo dataset,
[doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7352172](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7352172)
(CC BY 4.0, downloadable).

**The extreme tail is a distinct phenomenon, not the median case.**
"Overwintering" or "zombie" fires smoulder through an entire winter and
re-emerge the following spring, with holdover extending up to 7–8 months.
Xu, W. et al. (2022), *"Overwintering fires in boreal forests,"*
Environmental Research Letters, 17, 045005,
[doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/ac59aa](https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/ac59aa).
This is real and citable, but it is the far tail of the gamma distribution,
not a typical case — do not let a "months" example stand in for the bulk of
the distribution, which is much shorter.

## 5. Prior art

The point of this section is defensive: for each system below, state plainly
what it already does, so the team does not claim it as new in front of a
judge who knows the field.

**Wotton & Martell lightning fire occurrence model.** Operational in Ontario
for roughly two decades, run by the province's fire management agency,
converting lightning-strike location and polarity plus fuel-moisture indices
into an ignition-probability surface. Confirmed operational in Ontario;
**operational use in Saskatchewan specifically could not be confirmed** —
only Ontario is documented. [doi.org/10.1139/x05-071](https://doi.org/10.1139/x05-071).
**Do not claim:** "predicting ignition probability from lightning plus fuel
moisture" as new — this exact idea has been running as a government
operational product for ~20 years.

**US Forest Service Potential Lightning Ignition (PLI).** Part of the
Wildland Fire Assessment System, fed by the Missoula Fire Sciences Lab. Daily
national map combining 24-hour cloud-to-ground lightning counts (split by
polarity) with a fuel-type/fuel-depth/100-hour-fuel-moisture "lightning
efficiency" layer, added in 1999 and independently verified in published USFS
research.
[research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/28599](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/28599),
[wfas.net](https://www.wfas.net). **Do not claim:** a national-scale, daily,
public lightning-plus-fuel-moisture ignition risk map as new — it has existed
and been verified since 1999.

**Pano AI.** Commercial company. Two rotating cameras per station (visible +
infrared, ~1-minute sweep), cloud AI trained on 50+ billion images, mandatory
human analyst verification before alerting a fire agency. Monitors ~30
million acres across the US, Canada and Australia; customers include Xcel
Energy and Washington State DNR.
[technologyreview.com/2024/10/01/1104375](https://www.technologyreview.com/2024/10/01/1104375/2024-climate-tech-companies-pano-ai-fire-detecting-ai/).
It is **purely optical/IR smoke-and-fire detection after ignition** — it has
no lightning-sensing capability. **Do not claim:** AI-verified camera-based
smoke detection as new, and be explicit that Pano AI detects fires after they
start burning, not the strikes that started them.

**ALERTWildfire / ALERTCalifornia.** Pan-tilt-zoom camera consortium (UC San
Diego, University of Nevada Reno, University of Oregon), 160+ cameras across
CA/NV/OR/WA/ID; the California-specific ALERTCalifornia expansion with CAL
FIRE runs 1,000+ cameras in-state alone, with AI-assisted smoke detection
claimed down to fires as small as ~2 m².
[alertcalifornia.org](https://alertcalifornia.org/). Also purely post-
ignition. **Do not claim:** a large camera network for early smoke detection
as new — this one has existed at scale since 2010 (Tahoe pilot) and now spans
five US states.

**Dryad Silvanet.** Solar-powered, mesh-networked gas sensors (VOC/CO
electrochemical, plus PM2.5) built to detect smouldering off-gases before
visible smoke, with the newest generation adding direct-to-satellite
backhaul. Documented deployment: a Lebanon pilot, 91 sensors + 2 gateways
over 78 hectares, running since January 2023; standard reference density is
~400 sensors + 6 gateways per 400–500 ha.
[dryad.net](https://www.dryad.net/),
[globenewswire.com/news-release/2023/12/18](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2023/12/18/2797950/0/en/Dryad-s-Silvanet-Detects-Unauthorized-Wildfire-in-Lebanon-in-30-Minutes-Prompting-Timely-Response-and-Resolution.html).
Broader deployment claims beyond Lebanon are company/press material, not
independently corroborated here. **Do not claim:** a distributed
environmental-sensor mesh for ultra-early fire detection as new — this
exists, is commercialized, and senses combustion chemistry directly (a
different, arguably more direct signal than acoustic/lightning inference).

**Electromagnetic lightning networks — NLDN, GLD360, WWLLN, ENTLN.** All
four already solve "where did lightning strike" at a precision and scale a
single-station acoustic watchtower cannot match, wherever they have coverage:

| Network | Operator | Coverage | Detection efficiency | Location accuracy | Access |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NLDN | Vaisala (commercial) | CONUS + 200–300 km offshore | >95% for CG flashes (vendor figure) | ~84 m–200 m depending on the vendor document consulted — **figures vary across Vaisala's own pages; verify against the live current page before quoting one number** | Commercial licence, price not public |
| GLD360 | Vaisala (commercial) | Global | ~70% for CG flashes | 5–10 km at launch, improved to ~1 km median (GLD360 4.0, 2020) | Commercial licence, price not public |
| WWLLN | University of Washington (academic consortium) | Global | ~25–35% of all strokes; >70% for strokes >40–50 kA | ~2–3 km in well-instrumented regions, up to ~5–10 km elsewhere | Nominal-cost access for non-member researchers |
| ENTLN | AEM / Earth Networks (commercial) | Global (1,600+ sensors) | ~97.5% total-flash (independent study) vs. higher vendor claims | <100 m (independent study) | Commercial |

**Do not claim:** "a way to detect where lightning strikes" as novel, at any
precision, anywhere these networks have coverage. Any legitimate novelty
claim has to sit downstream of strike location — in continuing-current
estimation, fuel-moisture fusion, first-responder triage, or the low-cost
local sensor node itself — never in "we can tell where lightning hit."

## 6. Acoustic ranging: what is physically possible

**Speed of sound and flash-to-bang.** Standard physics: v ≈ 331 m/s at 0°C,
rising ~0.6 m/s per °C, giving the commonly used 343 m/s at 20°C. This part
is textbook and needs no further citation
([Physics LibreTexts §17.3](https://phys.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/University_Physics/University_Physics_(OpenStax)/Book:_University_Physics_I_-_Mechanics_Sound_Oscillations_and_Waves_(OpenStax)/17:_Sound/17.03:_Speed_of_Sound)).

**Audibility limit.** NOAA states thunder is "normally" audible only up to
about 10 miles (16 km) — and since lightning can strike outward from a storm
up to that same distance, hearing thunder at all means you are within
striking range.
[noaa.gov/jetstream/lightning/sound-of-thunder](https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/lightning/sound-of-thunder).
The spec's "roughly 15–20 km" figure is consistent with this NOAA range and
with the wider literature; use "up to ~20 km under favourable conditions,
NOAA's own baseline is 16 km" rather than a single hard number.

**The "~10% distance error" figure is unverified as a formal measurement.**
No NOAA/NWS source or peer-reviewed acoustics paper was found stating a
specific error percentage for flash-to-bang ranging. What is independently
verifiable: the common "count seconds, divide by five" rule is itself
approximate (the more exact speed-of-sound-based divisor is closer to 4.7 in
warm air), which alone introduces a systematic **~6% underestimate of
distance**, biased toward "the storm is closer than it is." **State the
mechanism (counting error + speed-of-sound approximation) rather than citing
an unsourced 10% figure.**

**Arthur Few's thunder acoustics.** Few, A.A., *"Thunder,"* Scientific
American — confirmed to exist and to be the foundational lay-audience source
on thunder acoustics (channel geometry, or "tortuosity," explains why
observers within a narrow cone perpendicular to the lightning channel hear a
sharp clap while others hear a longer rumble).
[scientificamerican.com/article/thunder](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/thunder/).
This document's author could not fetch the full article text (only a search
summary), so the exact publication date and any specific numeric claims
inside it are **not verified** — read the article directly before quoting a
number from it.

**Microphone-array bearing estimation is an established technique, not a
novel one.** Time-difference-of-arrival with cross-correlation (commonly
GCC-PHAT) is standard acoustic array signal processing, used operationally
for impulsive transient localization such as gunshot detection — a close
analogue to a thunder clap. A drone-mounted microphone-array shooter-
localization study reports mean accuracy of 0.87 m at 35 m range and ~7 m at
100 m range (a different domain, but useful as an order-of-magnitude
comparator for near-field acoustic ranging maturity).
[mdpi.com/1424-8220/19/19/4271](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/19/19/4271).
More directly relevant: a four-element broadband microphone array in central
New Mexico has been used to locate thunder sources in three dimensions via
slowness-search ranging —
[arXiv:1701.02968](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02968), "Mapping lightning in
the sky with a mini array" — the closest published precedent to this
project's own array concept. Related infrasound-array literature: Los Alamos
Sferic Array (Smith et al., 2002, JGR Atmospheres), and Park et al. (2014),
JGR, on automatic infrasound detection/location in the western US.

**Attenuation, refraction, and shadow zones are real and well documented —
for atmosphere, not confirmed for terrain.** In daytime "lapse" conditions
(temperature falling with height), sound refracts upward, away from the
ground, producing shadow zones where thunder is inaudible even though the
flash is visible — a commonly cited example places a shadow zone around
22.5 km (14 mi) from a ~4 km-tall lightning channel.
[Penn State acoustics, D. Russell — refraction of sound waves](https://www.acs.psu.edu/drussell/demos/refract/refract.html).
At night, temperature inversions do the opposite — bending sound back toward
the ground, which is why thunder often sounds louder and carries further in
cold weather.
[Acoustics Today — Refraction of Sound in the Atmosphere](https://acousticstoday.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/Article_1of4_from_ATCODK_2_2.pdf)
is the most rigorous source found for this mechanism. **A dedicated source
quantifying terrain (as opposed to atmospheric) shadow-zone effects on
thunder specifically was not found** — the terrain-blocking claim is
physically reasonable (it's the same acoustic-shadow principle applied to a
solid obstruction instead of a temperature gradient) but should be stated as
inference from established acoustics, not as a separately measured result.

**Where electromagnetic networks beat acoustics — stated plainly.** NLDN and
GLD360 operate at sub-kilometre-to-single-kilometre accuracy with no range
limit inside their coverage footprint (see §5 table). A single-station
acoustic rig tops out near NOAA's ~16–20 km audibility ceiling with error
sources (counting precision, refraction, terrain) that a fixed percentage
figure cannot honestly summarize yet. **Acoustic ranging is a genuine local
confirmation layer, not a competitor to EM lightning networks where those
networks have coverage** — say that outright rather than letting a judge
infer it.

## 8. What we can and cannot claim

| We may say | We may not say |
|---|---|
| Ignition probability per cloud-to-ground strike varies by roughly an order of magnitude across Canadian ecozones — computed here at ~1 fire per 105 flashes (Montane Cordillera) to ~1 per 836 (Boreal Plains), from Coogan et al. 2025 raw flash/fire counts. | That this ratio is fixed, universal, or independent of season and drought state — the same paper shows day-of-year and drought indices are top predictors, meaning the ratio moves through a season. |
| Duff moisture and drought indices (DMC, DC) are the dominant predictors of ignition, ranked above fuel type in Wotton & Martell (2005) and Coogan et al. (2025) across nearly every ecozone studied. | That there is a validated sharp threshold (e.g. "DMC = 20") that switches ignition on — a 2025 IJWF study found no such threshold; probability rises continuously. |
| Dry lightning is disproportionately fire-causing: ~20% of strikes, ~40% of fire counts, per Peterson et al. (2010) — a verified 2x overrepresentation. | That dry lightning ignites "30–50% more often" — that specific figure could not be traced to any primary source and should not be used. |
| Continuing current, not peak current, is the ignition mechanism — a return stroke is <10 ms and cannot transfer enough heat; long continuing current (tens to hundreds of ms) can. The ≥40 ms threshold traces to Fuquay (1972), consistently cited by later work. | That the threshold is specifically "100–200 A" — no primary source for that amperage range was found; secondary paraphrases disagree with each other. |
| Positive polarity correlates with higher ignition probability — Pérez-Invernón et al. (2021) confirm a "slightly higher" ignition probability for positive strokes, citing Latham & Williams (2001). | That positive strokes carry long continuing current "~80% of the time against ~10% for negative" — unverified at these values. The best-supported figures are ~20–50% for negative strokes (Schultz et al. 2019), with positive only qualitatively "higher," not quantified — and in the largest matched-to-real-fires dataset, 90% of fire-igniting strokes were still negative polarity, because negative strokes vastly outnumber positive ones. |
| Holdover time — strike to detectable fire — is real, wide, and right-skewed: a global database of 152,375 lightning-ignited wildfires across 13 countries (1921–2020) shows raw intervals from 1 minute to 87 days, best fit by a gamma-family distribution (Moris et al. 2023, 2024). | A fixed "hours before smoke" lead time, or a specific median/percentile — no verified median or percentile figure exists in what was retrieved. Report the distribution's shape and range, not a point estimate. Also: this 152,375 figure is global, not Canada-specific — say so. |
| Acoustic bearing and flash-to-bang ranging are established, real physics — TDOA/GCC-PHAT array processing is a mature technique (used for gunshot localization and thunder-array research), and thunder is audible to roughly NOAA's own 16–20 km ceiling. | That acoustic ranging beats, matches, or is unaffected by electromagnetic lightning networks where they have coverage — NLDN/GLD360 operate at sub-kilometre-to-~1 km accuracy with no range limit; acoustics is a local confirmation layer, not a competitor, and a specific "~10% distance error" figure for flash-to-bang is unverified — describe the error sources (counting precision, atmospheric refraction, terrain) instead of citing an unsourced percentage. |
| The Canadian 2023 season is exceptional and lightning-driven: 59% of fires, 93% of area burned, despite the third-lowest lightning-detection count since 1998 — driven by fuel dryness, not more lightning. Quebec alone burned 4.3 million hectares, 99.9% lightning-caused, a century record exceeding the prior 20 years combined, and its smoke reached New York with an AQI ≥465. | That 2023 proves lightning frequency is increasing, or that this is a typical year — the paper explicitly shows lightning detections were unusually *low* that year; the record is a fuel-dryness story. |
| Wotton & Martell's model has been operational in Ontario for roughly two decades; the US Forest Service has published verified Potential Lightning Ignition maps since 1999; Pano AI, ALERTWildfire/ALERTCalifornia, and Dryad Silvanet are real, deployed, commercial or academic systems solving adjacent problems (post-ignition optical detection, or gas-sensing pre-visible-smoke detection). | Novelty of the underlying ignition science, of camera-based post-ignition detection, or of gas-sensor mesh detection — all of that already exists. The novelty claim, if any, has to be in the specific fusion (live strike data + continuing-current estimation + fuel moisture, into a first-responder triage product) and in the site-placement optimisation, not in any single sensing modality. |
| EM lightning networks (NLDN, GLD360, WWLLN, ENTLN) already solve strike location precisely where they have coverage. | That this project invents "a way to detect where lightning strikes." It doesn't, and claiming so is the single fastest way to lose a technical judge. |
