How a position is chosen

Risk multiplies fuel by hazard: land-cover flammability from ESA WorldCover at 10 m gates everything, so no amount of fire weather can put a sensor in a lake. On top of that sit observed activity — NASA FIRMS thermal detections weighted by radiative power — and fire weather from the Canadian FWI System computed on ERA5 reanalysis, run as a proper sequential chain of moisture codes rather than evaluated a day at a time.

Placement is Poisson-disk (blue noise) with the minimum spacing made a function of that risk field. Blue noise matters here: points are irregular, so the network has no lattice direction a fire front can slip along, yet no two nodes are ever closer than the local radius, so nothing clusters and no hardware is wasted. Uniform random placement clumps and leaves holes; a grid has the lattice problem. The candidate pool is deliberately denser than the answer, because the minimiser can only remove nodes — and discs of radius R only tile the plane at hexagonal spacing R√3, so a sparser pool leaves gaps no pruning can close.

Minimisation is greedy set cover: repeatedly take the node adding the most still-uncovered risk-weighted demand. That is not just convenient — for maximum coverage greedy is provably within (1 − 1/e) ≈ 63% of optimal, and no polynomial algorithm beats that bound unless P = NP.

What this is and is not. Each forest gets a sentinel budget of a few nodes, so coverage is deliberately partial: ten 2 km sensors watch about 2% of a 5,500 km² forest. The numbers above report that honestly rather than flattering it — the nodes are placed to watch the most risk available for the budget, not to blanket the ground. The sweep samples the land surface on a regular grid rather than tiling every hectare, so a forest smaller than the grid step can fall between cells. The fire-weather window also spans January–July, which sits outside the fire season for southern-hemisphere and boreal sites, so their FWI understates the true peak.
